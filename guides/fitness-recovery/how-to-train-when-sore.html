How to train when you are sore

Short answer: do not use soreness as the only signal. Check movement quality and energy, warm up carefully, then reduce the session, choose another focus or rest if the planned work does not feel right.

By Antonin Weissgerber · Last updated August 6, 2026

Soreness is information, not an instruction

Post-workout soreness can tell you that a muscle experienced unfamiliar or demanding work, but it does not automatically mean you must skip every session. It also does not prove that a muscle is fully ready. Treat it as one piece of context alongside movement, control, energy and pain.

Start with the planned movement

Use a gradual warm-up and pay attention to range of motion and technique. If the movement feels normal and the soreness is mild, you may choose to continue with less volume or intensity. If technique changes or the movement feels painful, switch the exercise, train another area or stop.

Adapt instead of abandoning the week

A flexible plan can preserve consistency without forcing a bad session. Move from heavy work to a lighter variation, replace a lower-body session with upper body, use mobility or take a rest day. The best choice depends on your training history and current condition, not on completing a calendar at any cost.

Track what happened

Note which muscle groups were trained, how difficult the session was and how you felt next time. Over time, that record can help you avoid repeating too much work before you are ready. Forma tracks training by muscle group and can suggest a different focus when recent work indicates fatigue.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I work out when I am sore?

It depends on how you feel and what the session involves. Consider movement quality, range of motion, energy and whether the exercise creates pain rather than ordinary soreness.

Should I train a different muscle group?

That can be reasonable if the planned movement feels comfortable and the rest of your body is ready.

When should I stop training?

Stop for sharp or unusual pain, worsening symptoms, loss of control or anything that concerns you, and seek qualified advice when appropriate.
